Here’s a **VC Analyst Math One-Pager** focused on the quantitative skills that actually show up in sourcing, diligence, financial modeling, and investment memos. # Venture Capital Analyst — Math One-Pager ### 1. Percentages & Growth **Growth rate** $$ g=\frac{X_t-X_0}{X_0} $$ **CAGR** $$ CAGR=\left(\frac{X_t}{X_0}\right)^{1/t}-1 $$ Example: Revenue grows from $2M → $8M in 3 years: $$ CAGR=(8/2)^{1/3}-1=58.7\% $$ **Rule of 72** $$ \text{Years to double}\approx\frac{72}{g} $$ At 24% growth → roughly 3 years to double. --- ### 2. Market Sizing **TAM** $$ TAM = \text{Potential Customers}\times\text{Annual Revenue/Customer} $$ **SAM** The portion of TAM the company can realistically serve. **SOM** The portion it could realistically capture. **Bottom-up example** $$ 1M\text{ customers}\times\$500/year=\$500M\ TAM $$ Think in terms of: > **Customers × Price × Frequency** --- ### 3. Unit Economics The core equations: $$ Gross\ Margin=\frac{Revenue-COGS}{Revenue} $$ $$ CAC=\frac{\text{Sales + Marketing Spend}}{\text{New Customers}} $$ $$ LTV\approx ARPU\times Gross\ Margin\times Customer\ Lifetime $$ A simple subscription approximation: $$ LTV\approx\frac{ARPU\times Gross\ Margin}{Monthly\ Churn} $$ **LTV/CAC** $$ \frac{LTV}{CAC} $$ **CAC Payback** $$ CAC\ Payback=\frac{CAC}{Monthly\ Gross\ Profit} $$ --- ### 4. SaaS Metrics **ARR** $$ ARR=MRR\times12 $$ **MRR Growth** $$ \frac{MRR_t-MRR_{t-1}}{MRR_{t-1}} $$ **Net Revenue Retention** $$ NRR= \frac{Beginning\ Revenue +Expansion-Churn-Contraction}{Beginning\ Revenue} $$ **Burn Multiple** $$ Burn\ Multiple= \frac{Net\ Burn}{Net\ New\ ARR} $$ This measures how much cash is being consumed to create incremental recurring revenue. --- ### 5. Startup Runway $$ Runway=\frac{Cash}{Monthly\ Net\ Burn} $$ Example: $6M cash / $500K monthly burn: $$ 12\text{ months} $$ If burn is increasing, use a **cash-flow forecast**, not this simple approximation. --- ### 6. Valuation **Post-money valuation** $$ Post=\frac{Investment}{Ownership\%} $$ Example: $5M invested for 20%: $$ Post=\frac{\$5M}{0.20}=\$25M $$ Then: $$ Pre=Post-Investment $$ $$ Pre=\$20M $$ --- ### 7. Dilution If a new investor receives 20% of the post-money company: Existing shareholders collectively retain: $$ 1-20\%=80\% $$ If you own 10% beforehand: $$ 10\%\times80\%=8\% $$ Your ownership becomes **8%**. --- ### 8. Venture Returns **MOIC** $$ MOIC=\frac{Exit\ Value}{Investment} $$ $10M → $100M: $$ MOIC=10\times $$ **IRR** For a single investment with no interim cash flows: $$ IRR=\left(\frac{Exit}{Investment}\right)^{1/t}-1 $$ 10× over 5 years: $$ IRR=10^{1/5}-1\approx58.5\% $$ --- ### 9. Ownership → Exit Proceeds Suppose a fund owns **8%** at exit and the company sells for **$500M**: $$ \$500M\times8\%=\$40M $$ But analysts must account for: * Dilution * Preferred stock * Liquidation preferences * Option pools * Convertible securities * Subsequent financing rounds --- ### 10. Expected Value VC is fundamentally a **probability-weighted returns problem**. $$ EV=\sum_i P_i\times Payoff_i $$ Example: | Outcome | Probability | Fund Proceeds | | ------------- | ----------: | ------------: | | Failure | 60% | $0 | | Moderate exit | 25% | $30M | | Huge exit | 15% | $200M | $$ EV=(.60)(0)+(.25)(30)+(.15)(200) $$ $$ EV=\$37.5M $$ The important insight: > **Venture portfolios are often driven by a small number of extreme outcomes.** --- # The Math Hierarchy If you're preparing for a **VC Analyst interview**, I'd prioritize: **Tier 1 — Must know** * Percentages * Growth rates * CAGR * Margins * Revenue * ARR/MRR * CAC * LTV * Burn * Runway * Dilution * Pre/post-money valuation * MOIC * IRR **Tier 2 — Very useful** * Probability-weighted returns * Cap tables * Ownership modeling * Cohort analysis * Retention * NRR * Sensitivity analysis * Scenario analysis **Tier 3 — Advanced** * DCF * Public-company comps * Precedent transactions * Monte Carlo simulation * Options/warrants * Convertible notes/SAFEs * Liquidation waterfalls * Portfolio construction * Power-law distributions ### The meta-skill The most important mathematical skill for a VC analyst isn't calculus. It's being able to look at a company and rapidly translate: $$ \boxed{ Customers \rightarrow Revenue \rightarrow Margins \rightarrow Cash Flow \rightarrow Valuation \rightarrow Ownership \rightarrow Exit \rightarrow Fund\ Return } $$ That chain is the **core quantitative language of venture capital**.
